# The making of Cecil Rhodes: vicarage to vision of world dominion

Cecil John Rhodes's 1877 "Confession of Faith" — a 24-year-old's blueprint for Anglo-Saxon world domination through a secret society modeled on the Jesuits — emerged from an identifiable and extraordinary confluence of forces: a vicar's sickly son, sent to Africa at seventeen to avoid an early grave, was simultaneously forged in the lawless crucible of Kimberley's diamond fields and steeped in Oxford's imperial ideology. **His confrontation with early mortality fused with Ruskinian idealism, Social Darwinist racial theory, Masonic organizational structure, and the ruthless capitalism of the frontier to produce the originating document of the Anglo-American power nexus.** What follows traces each thread to its source.

---

## The vicarage: brick money, banking connections, and a pragmatic faith

Cecil John Rhodes was born on July 5, 1853, not in the vicarage itself but in a large house called Netteswell (or Thorley Bourne) on the London Road in Bishop's Stortford, Hertfordshire — now the town's museum. His father, Reverend Francis William Rhodes, was vicar of St Michael's Church from 1849 to 1876, a man known for never preaching a sermon longer than ten minutes. Cecil described him as "coolly pragmatic," a father who interrogated his son's dreams and encouraged him to rebuild them on "more practical lines."

No source explicitly categorizes the elder Rhodes's churchmanship, but the circumstantial evidence points firmly toward **Broad Church or conventional Low Church** Anglicanism — moderate, practical, unostentatious. His famous brevity in preaching would have been unusual for an evangelical Low Churchman who placed extended sermons at the heart of worship. He had no connection to the Tractarian High Church movement. His family background was commercial, not aristocratic or high-clerical: Cecil's paternal grandfather, William Rhodes of Leyton Grange, Essex, was a brick manufacturer in Hackney and Dalston who patented improvements to brickmaking. The family were, in biographer Ian Colvin's phrase, "substantial people of St. Pancras Parish — yeomen, graziers, cowkeepers, brickmakers, churchwardens."

The maternal side brought more distinguished connections. Cecil's mother, Louisa Peacock, came from one of the most prominent families in southern Lincolnshire. Her grandfather, Anthony Peacock, co-founded the **Sleaford and Newark Bank** in 1792 — later absorbed by Lloyds Bank in 1912 — and sponsored the construction of the Sleaford Navigation canal. Her father, Anthony Taylor Peacock, became a Member of Parliament in the 1850s. Louisa was described as "a warm, cheerful woman" with an especially close relationship to Cecil, in contrast to his more distant rapport with his father. She died on November 1, 1873, while Cecil was at Oxford.

A crucial figure was Aunt Sophia Peacock — Louisa's unmarried sister who lived at the Manor House in Sleaford. She was Cecil's godmother, funded his passage to Africa (lending approximately £2,000–£3,000), and hosted him regularly during holidays. Without Sophia's money, Cecil might never have reached South Africa.

The family occupied a specific Victorian class position: **upper professional-commercial middle class**, bridging gentry and trade. The vicar was prosperous enough to send one son to Eton, another to Winchester, and three into the army. The family's wealth derived from London property, brick manufacturing, and the Peacock banking connection. They were not aristocrats, but they inhabited the social zone that sent sons to public schools and aspired to gentility. No evidence exists of any Masonic or esoteric connections in the family before Cecil's own initiation in 1877.

## The brothers who never married: a remarkable family pattern

Louisa bore eleven children, of whom nine survived infancy. Including Elizabeth, Francis's daughter from his first wife (who died in childbirth in 1835), the household contained at least ten children. **Not one of them ever married** — a fact so extraordinary that multiple biographers remark upon it without fully explaining it. The vicar wanted his sons to enter the Church, but "they had their own views, and most of them chose the Army."

Four brothers — Frank, Elmhirst, Arthur Montagu, and Bernard Maitland — pursued military careers, fighting across Egypt, Sudan, and southern Africa. Colonel Frank Rhodes was arrested for his role in the Jameson Raid of 1895, convicted of high treason, and nearly executed. Ernest Frederick served in the Royal Engineers. The military-colonial orientation of the family was pronounced: the vicarage produced not clergymen but soldiers and empire-builders.

Herbert Rhodes, the eldest son, was the pivot upon which Cecil's entire trajectory turned. "A rover by nature," Herbert emigrated to Natal and received a 200-acre grant for cotton farming in the Umkomaas Valley. When Cecil arrived in September 1870, he found the farm "in a state of disrepair, contrary to Herbert's reports to their aunt." Herbert was frequently absent for horse-racing and cricket in Pietermaritzburg. In March 1871, he abandoned the farm for the diamond fields, taking the best oxen. By November 1871, he had handed his diamond claims to Cecil and departed. The two brothers never saw each other again. Herbert drifted northward, prospecting for gold in the eastern Transvaal and exploring the interior. In 1879, he died in what is now Malawi when **a barrel of spirits exploded at his campfire and burned him to death** — a gruesome end suggesting problems with alcohol.

Herbert's pattern of restless abandonment paradoxically created Cecil's fortune. Each time Herbert left, Cecil was forced to take responsibility — first the cotton farm, then the diamond claims. Herbert's irresponsibility was the negative template against which Cecil built his relentless discipline.

## The heart, not the lungs: a misdiagnosis that changed history

The popular narrative held that Cecil was sent to South Africa because of weak lungs and suspected tuberculosis. The truth was more complex and more consequential. In 1869, Mrs. Rhodes took her sixteen-year-old son to the family doctor after alarming symptoms: "fatigue no longer blanched his lips — it turned them livid, and exertion was followed continually by alarming faintness." The doctor examined him and **pronounced the lungs sound**. The problem was his heart: "not actually diseased" but "seriously overtaxed, labouring quite inadequately to meet the demands of his rapid growth."

Modern retrospective analysis by J.C. Shee, published in the *Central African Journal of Medicine* in 1965, identified Rhodes's lifelong condition as almost certainly **congenital heart disease — most probably an atrial septal defect**. His autopsy in 1902 revealed an enormously thickened pericardium adherent to the heart, but no healed tuberculosis. Yet the TB narrative persisted because Victorian medicine prescribed warm, dry climates and sea voyages for lung complaints, and this justified the African exile.

Rhodes suffered his first heart attack at nineteen, in July 1872 at Kimberley, and could not dig for eight months. A more severe attack struck in 1877, at twenty-four, which prompted him to write both the Confession of Faith and his first will. **The awareness of imminent death drove everything.** "Everything is too short," he once said. "From the cradle to the grave, what is it? Three days at the seaside." This sense of compressed time — the urgency of a man racing his own failing heart — explains the grandiosity of the 1877 vision. It was not the fantasy of someone with unlimited horizons but the desperate ambition of someone who believed he had almost none.

Victorian "muscular Christianity" — the ideology of physical vigor as moral virtue, popularized by Charles Kingsley and Thomas Hughes — made Cecil's condition doubly isolating. While his brothers were robust enough for Eton, Winchester, and the army, Cecil was kept home at the local grammar school because of his health. He was described as "a golden-haired, delicate-looking little fellow, in nature somewhat shy and retiring." The exclusion from public school denied him the social networks and institutional cachet his brothers enjoyed. The compensatory drive to prove himself — to build something so vast it would dwarf any military career — seems psychologically inevitable.

## From grammar school to diamond fields: the education of an empire-builder

Cecil attended the Bishop's Stortford Grammar School, founded in 1579 and revived by his own father in 1850. The curriculum was standard classical fare — Latin, Greek, Scripture, mathematics — at a level significantly below the great public schools. He was "an active but unremarkable pupil," though he took a classical scholarship and made the school's first eleven at cricket by thirteen. The key marker of his intellectual formation was not the curriculum but his independent reading: **Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations* became his lifelong companion**, and he devoured Edward Gibbon's *Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*. The Stoic philosopher-emperor and the grand narrative of imperial rise and collapse shaped his imagination long before Oxford.

He left school in 1869 when his health failed and sailed for Natal in June 1870 aboard the wooden ship *Eudora*, arriving in Durban on September 1 after a seventy-two-day voyage. He was seventeen, funded by Aunt Sophia, carrying classical volumes and a Greek lexicon alongside his pick and spades.

The cotton venture failed. By October 1871, Cecil followed Herbert to the Kimberley diamond fields, traveling by Scotch cart drawn by oxen. What he found was a vision "to rival Dante's description of hell" — an enormous open pit with hundreds of sweating diggers, black and white, climbing rickety ladders into a deepening abyss. Nearly **50,000 people** inhabited a sprawling, polyglot camp characterized by lawless frontier capitalism, racial conflict, get-rich-quick frenzy, and devastating mortality from mining accidents, disease, and violence. It was the largest urban society in the interior of southern Africa.

Rhodes's ascent was methodical rather than spectacular. He managed Herbert's claims, then partnered with **Charles Dunell Rudd** — a Harrow-and-Cambridge man nine years his senior — in a brilliant sequence of ventures: importing a steam-powered ice machine to sell refreshments to miners, then securing contracts to pump floodwater from the three main mines at £400–£500 per month. Crucially, they accepted **mine claims as payment** for pumping services, systematically accumulating holdings while others went bankrupt. During the depression of 1874–75, they bought flooded and abandoned claims cheaply, understanding what most diggers did not: the "blue ground" below the surface still contained diamonds. By 1880, they launched the De Beers Mining Company with £200,000 in capital. The Rothschild connection came later, in 1887, when N.M. Rothschild & Sons financed the great consolidation.

The diamond fields taught Rhodes three things that conventional education never could: that **monopoly was the only structural solution** to volatile commodity markets, that every man had his price, and that power over African labor was the foundation of southern African wealth. Even as a teenager managing the cotton farm, he had supervised Zulu laborers. At Kimberley, the compound system, pass laws, and systematic searching of African workers created the template for later apartheid labor systems. Rhodes was, as biographer William Kelleher Storey argues, fundamentally "a specialist in the disassembly of whole lands and societies for resource extraction."

## Oxford, Ruskin, and the intellectual architecture of supremacism

Rhodes matriculated at Oriel College, Oxford, on October 13, 1873 — three years after arriving in Africa, already a working diamond miner. He was older than typical undergraduates, wealthier from trade (a socially suspect source), and frequently absent. He would pass around a small box of diamonds in class. He shuttled between Kimberley and Oxford for eight years, finally taking his BA in 1881 — almost certainly a Pass degree rather than Honours, given his intermittent attendance. His determination to get the degree was driven by a specific conviction: "Wherever you turn your eye — except in science — an Oxford man is at the top of the tree."

Oriel in the 1870s was past its intellectual prime. Once the cradle of the Oxford Movement, it was now "better known for prowess at rowing, football and cricket than in final exams." But the broader Oxford atmosphere was fermenting with imperial ideology. The key influences on Rhodes were layered and reinforcing:

**John Ruskin's inaugural lecture** as Slade Professor of Fine Art, delivered February 8, 1870, to an overflow crowd at the Sheldonian Theatre, contained a passage that became Rhodes's ideological lodestar: "There is a destiny now possible to us — the highest ever set before a nation to be accepted or refused. We are still undegenerate in race; a race mingled of the best northern blood... She must found colonies as fast and as far as she is able, formed of her most energetic and worthiest men; seizing every piece of fruitful waste ground she can set her foot on." Rhodes almost certainly did **not** attend this lecture — he was in England but did not arrive at Oxford until 1873. He encountered the published text, issued by Clarendon Press in 1872. Carroll Quigley's claim that Rhodes "copied it out in longhand and kept it with him for thirty years" lacks independent verification, but the ideological parallels are unmistakable: Ruskin's "found colonies... formed of her most energetic and worthiest men" maps directly onto Rhodes's "secret society" of the ablest.

**Winwood Reade's *The Martyrdom of Man*** (1872) was, by Rhodes's own testimony, the most transformative single text. "It made me what I am," he told associates. This controversial universal history applied Darwinian evolution to civilization, arguing that war, slavery, and religion were necessary developmental stages to be superseded by science. It was denounced by Gladstone as dangerously irreligious. For Rhodes, it provided the framework for replacing Christian faith with a secular religion of racial progress — and it centered Africa in the story of human development.

**Aristotle's concept of *megalopsychia*** — greatness of soul — appears twice in the Confession, where Rhodes laments that men possessing this quality lack the organizational channel to exercise it. **Marcus Aurelius's Stoic ethics** of duty and self-mastery remained his constant companion. **Gibbon's imperial narrative** provided the template of civilizational rise and fall. **Charles Dilke's *Greater Britain*** (1868), a bestselling travelogue, introduced the concept of a global Anglo-Saxon community: "I followed England round the world... the grandeur of our race, already girding the earth."

The intellectual atmosphere of 1870s Oxford combined all these currents. The Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71 had intensified British anxiety about national power. Social Darwinism was ascendant. The historian Edward Augustus Freeman was articulating Anglo-Saxon racial historiography. Benjamin Jowett, Master of Balliol, preached his desire to "inoculate the world" with Oxford-educated leaders. The "New Imperialism" — a shift from mid-Victorian ambivalence to active enthusiasm for expansion — was gathering force.

## The Masonic initiation and the Confession of Faith

The year 1877 was the hinge. Rhodes was initiated into the **Apollo University Lodge No. 357** — the principal Masonic lodge for Oxford members — on January 30, 1877. He was raised to Master Mason on April 17. On June 2, he became a life member and joined the **Prince Rose Croix Lodge No. 30** (the eighteenth degree in the Scottish Rite), paying five pounds ten shillings. That same day — or very close to it — he composed the Confession of Faith.

Apollo Lodge was no marginal institution. Its membership read as a roster of the British imperial establishment: the Prince of Wales (later Edward VII), Prince Leopold, **Oscar Wilde** (initiated 1875, two years before Rhodes), the Earl of Carnarvon (Secretary of State for the Colonies, whom Rhodes named as trustee of his first will), and a succession of governors-general, colonial administrators, and senior politicians. Between its founding in 1819 and 1902, the lodge initiated 1,271 members.

Rhodes drew from Freemasonry its **organizational architecture** while despising its purposelessness. In the Confession, he wrote: "I become a member of the Masonic order. I see the wealth and power they possess, the influence they hold, and I think over their ceremonies, and I wonder that a large body of men can devote themselves to what at times appear the most ridiculous and absurd rites without an object and without an end." His secret society would take Masonic structure — concentric circles of initiation, oaths of secrecy, graded testing of recruits — and direct it toward a specific imperial object.

The **Jesuit model** was equally explicit. "I look into history and I read the story of the Jesuits," he wrote. "I see what they were able to do in a bad cause and I might say under bad leaders." Thirteen years later, in 1890, he instructed a trustee: "Take Constitution of the Jesuits if obtainable and insert 'English Empire' for 'Roman Catholic Religion.'" The features he wanted to emulate were specific: centralized monarchical governance (a General at the apex), lifetime commitment through binding oaths, deployment of members at the will of superiors, embedding in key institutions (universities, legislatures, the press), and recruitment of the talented regardless of class. The Jesuit concept of *perinde ac cadaver* obedience — total submission to the order — informed the total commitment he demanded of his recruits.

The Confession itself opens with a question borrowed from classical philosophy: "It often strikes a man to inquire what is the chief good in life." Rhodes's answer is racial mission. **"I contend that we are the finest race in the world and that the more of the world we inhabit the better it is for the human race."** He laments the loss of America — "two or three ignorant pig-headed statesmen" cost Britain millions of potential English births — and proposes: "Why should we not form a secret society with but one object: the furtherance of the British Empire and the bringing of the whole uncivilised world under British rule, for the recovery of the United States, for the making the Anglo-Saxon race but one Empire." Members would be placed at universities to identify one recruit "perhaps in every thousand," tested for endurance and eloquence, bound by oath to lifetime service, and deployed wherever needed. The society should "inspire and even own portions of the press, for the press rules the mind of the people."

Three months later, on September 19, 1877, Rhodes formalized this in his first will, leaving his estate (then about £10,000) to Lord Carnarvon and Sidney Shippard for the establishment of a secret society whose objects included "the occupation by British settlers of the entire Continent of Africa, the Holy Land, the Valley of the Euphrates... the whole of South America, the Islands of the Pacific... **the ultimate recovery of the United States of America as an integral part of the British Empire**... and, finally, the foundation of so great a Power as to render wars impossible."

## The inner life: Pickering, the lambs, and the flight from women

Rhodes never married. The most authoritative biographer, Robert Rotberg, collaborated with Miles F. Shore, Bullard Professor of Psychiatry at Harvard, to produce a psychobiography that calls Rhodes's homosexuality **"indisputable on the basis of the available evidence."** Other scholars are more cautious, noting the evidence is "inferred, but not proven" — Rhodes left no diaries and destroyed much personal correspondence before his death.

The relationship with **Neville Pickering** is the most documented. Pickering, the son of a pastor, was hired in 1881 as secretary of the De Beers Mining Company. Graham Bower, the imperial secretary in Cape Town, described theirs as "an absolutely lover-like friendship." They lived together from 1882 to 1886. In October 1882, Rhodes made Pickering his sole heir. When Pickering suffered a riding accident in June 1884, Rhodes abandoned business negotiations on the fabulously rich Witwatersrand goldfields, riding 300 miles on top of mailbags for fifteen hours to reach his bedside. Pickering died in Rhodes's arms on October 18, 1886. At the funeral, Rhodes wept uncontrollably, punctuated by bursts of high-pitched, maniacal laughter. He never again entered the house they had shared. His devotion reportedly cost him gold claims eventually worth £250 million.

After Pickering's death, Rhodes surrounded himself with a succession of young male secretaries known as "Rhodes's lambs" — Harry Currey, Philip Jourdan, Johnny Grimmer, Robert Coryndon. When Currey announced his engagement in 1894, Rhodes flew into a rage and demanded he vacate his house. Jourdan later wrote that Rhodes "seemed to have a liking for young men" and was "particularly partial to people with blue eyes." Rhodes created an entirely male domestic world, "entertaining only men and employing only male servants." His closest lifelong companion was **Leander Starr Jameson** ("Dr. Jim"), with whom he shared a bungalow in Kimberley. Neither man ever married. Jameson nursed Rhodes through his final illness, lived in his mansion after his death, and in 1920 was reinterred beside Rhodes on Malindidzimu Hill in the Matopos.

When asked by Queen Victoria whether it was true he hated women, Rhodes replied diplomatically that he could hardly hate the sex to which she belonged. To friends he was blunter: "I hope you won't get married. I hate people getting married, they simply become machines and have no ideas beyond their respective spouses and offspring." The all-male secret society, the all-male domestic sphere, the Rhodes Scholarships restricted to men until 1976 with their requirement of "fondness for success in manly outdoor sports" — the gendered architecture of Rhodes's vision is unmistakable and deeply personal.

## From vicarage faith to the religion of race

Rhodes grew up steeped in Anglican worship — his father seated the seven boys in carved choir stalls he called "the seven angels of the seven churches." But W.T. Stead, who knew Rhodes intimately, wrote: "Mr. Rhodes is not, in the ordinary sense of the word, a religious man. He was born in a rectory, and, like many other clergymen's sons, he is no great Churchman. He has an exaggerated idea of the extent to which modern research has pulverised the authority of the Bible."

The decisive rupture came through **Winwood Reade**, whose *Martyrdom of Man* Rhodes read shortly after its 1872 publication. He described it as "creepy" and as the book that "made me what I am." Its Darwinian framework dissolved his residual Christianity and replaced it with what can only be called a **religion of racial evolution**. The "creed" Stead attributed to Rhodes ran: he "believes in the gospel of evolution, of the survival of the fittest, of progress by natural selection... The perfecting of the fittest species among the animals, or of races among men, and then the conferring upon the perfected species or race the title-deeds of the future — that seemed to Mr. Rhodes, through his Darwinian spectacles, the way in which God is governing His world."

Rhodes himself said: "If there be a God, I think what He would like me to do is to paint as much of the map of Africa British red as possible." This is not atheism but a transmutation of faith — the imperial mission replacing salvation, the secret society replacing the Church, Anglo-Saxon supremacy replacing the Kingdom of God. The Confession's language is saturated with quasi-religious structure: it proposes forming "a Church for the extension of the British Empire," demands members be "bound by oath to serve for the rest of his life," and envisions a messianic culmination in "the foundation of so great a Power as to render wars impossible." Stead captured the paradox perfectly: "If you could imagine an emperor of old Rome crossed with one of Cromwell's Ironsides, and the result brought up at the feet of Ignatius Loyola, you would have an amalgam not unlike that which men call Cecil Rhodes."

The cosmic scale of his ambition revealed the depth of the substitution: "The world is nearly all parcelled out, and what there is left of it is being divided up, conquered and colonised. To think of these stars that you see overhead at night, these vast worlds which we can never reach. I would annex the planets if I could; I often think of that. It makes me sad to see them so clear and yet so far."

## The psychological architecture: what the biographers found

Rotberg and Shore's psychobiography — the product of seventeen years' research and a collaboration between a historian and Harvard's Bullard Professor of Psychiatry — identifies the psychological engine driving Rhodes's vision as a **fusion of mortality-awareness with compensatory grandiosity**. The sickly boy excluded from public school and the army, told at nineteen he might have six months to live, constructed an ambition vast enough to transcend his own biological limitations. His heart was failing; he would build something that outlived the body.

Key psychological traits identified across the major biographies converge on a consistent portrait:

- **Grandiosity and megalomaniacal scope** — from the Confession to "I would annex the planets," from the first will's catalog of continents to his desire that his name endure four thousand years
- **Charm fused with ruthlessness** — "every man has his price" coexisted with genuine warmth toward protégés and an ability to inspire quasi-religious devotion in followers
- **Narcissistic control** — he demanded associates live with him, raged when they chose to marry, and organized his domestic and political life around total loyalty
- **Emotional volatility** — the falsetto outbursts, the "maniacal laughter" at Pickering's funeral, the oscillation between brooding silence and impassioned monologue
- **The mortality engine** — "Everything is too short. From the cradle to the grave, what is it? Three days at the seaside." His deathbed words — **"So little done, so much to do"** — confirmed a drive that death itself could not satisfy

Sarah Gertrude Millin, writing as fascism rose in Europe, recognized what she was looking at: "the something that rises in me against Rhodes is the something — unendingly intensified — that rises in me against Germany." Stuart Cloete called him "the prototype of the modern dictator," constructing "a secret society ruling a Great British *reich*." G.K. Chesterton, his contemporary, was harsher still: "What he called his ideals were the dregs of a Darwinism which had already grown not only stagnant, but poisonous... it was exactly because he had no ideas to spread that he invoked slaughter, violated justice and ruined republics to spread them."

## Conclusion: the formula that produced the vision

The 1877 Confession of Faith was not the product of a single influence but of a specific, unrepeatable chemical reaction. A vicar's sickly fifth son, excluded from the masculine world of public schools and the military, was exiled to Africa at seventeen with a failing heart and classical texts. In the lawless crucible of Kimberley, he discovered that raw capitalism rewarded strategic ruthlessness over physical vigor — that his particular gifts of long-term vision and systematic consolidation could defeat stronger men. Shuttling between the diamond pit and Oxford's dreaming spires, he encountered Ruskin's call for England to "found colonies as fast and as far as she is able," Reade's Social Darwinist replacement for Christianity, Aristotle's concept of the great-souled man, and the organizational models of the Jesuits and Freemasons. His congenital heart condition — misdiagnosed as tuberculosis, producing repeated near-death episodes from age nineteen — injected existential urgency into everything.

The resulting document fused **religious structure** (the vicarage childhood) with **secular content** (Darwinian racial theory), **organizational form** (Jesuit hierarchy and Masonic degrees) with **imperial purpose** (Ruskinian colonialism), and **personal compensation** (the sickly boy's will to transcend bodily limitation) with **world-historical ambition** (Anglo-Saxon global supremacy). The secret society Rhodes envisioned — formally constituted on February 5, 1891, with Rhodes as General, W.T. Stead and Lord Rothschild as successors, and a structure of Junta, Circle of Initiates, and Association of Helpers — eventually metamorphosed through the Round Table movement, the Royal Institute of International Affairs (Chatham House, 1919), and the Council on Foreign Relations (1921) into the institutional architecture of the Anglo-American power nexus.

Rhodes died on March 26, 1902, at forty-eight, his heart finally giving out in a seaside cottage in Muizenberg. He had made seven wills, each refining the vision from clandestine conspiracy to overt mechanism — the Rhodes Scholarships, designed to recruit promising young men from America, the colonies, and Germany to Oxford, achieving through education what the secret society would have achieved through conspiracy: the creation of an Anglo-Saxon global elite sharing common values and networks. The formula that produced this vision — mortality plus megalomania, vicarage piety transmuted into racial mission, diamond-field ruthlessness married to Oxonian idealism — was Cecil Rhodes's singular contribution to history. Understanding its components is essential to understanding the system it set in motion.
